Research

We study what machine-learned representations capture about biology, where they break down, and how to build reliable computational methods to analyze, evaluate, and design biological molecules.

Theme 01

Representation learning for biology

Self-supervised models for protein and nucleotide sequences.

In SeqVec and ProtTrans, we trained self-supervised language models on large protein sequence databases without structural or evolutionary labels. Representations from ProtT5 matched or exceeded alignment-based predictors on secondary structure, subcellular localization, and per-residue conservation tasks at much lower inference cost, and we packaged embedding extraction and downstream prediction workflows in bio_embeddings.

In Nucleotide Transformer, we trained foundation models across human and multispecies genomes to predict regulatory elements, splice sites, and variant effects directly from DNA sequence. Related genome-scale language modeling in GenSLMs tracked the evolutionary dynamics of SARS-CoV-2.

We also integrated protein language model predictors into PredictProtein, adding alignment-free single-sequence structure and function annotations to a web server operating since 1992.

Theme 02

Generative and programmable design

Atomistic generative models for proteins, binders, and multi-component biomolecules.

We develop generative models for de novo protein and biomolecular design, focusing on atomistic latent representations, synthetic pretraining data, and inference-time search.

Across a sequence of models, we extended flow-based structure generation from protein backbones to full-atom complexes. Proteina scales transformer flow matching with hierarchical fold-class conditioning to generate designable backbones up to 800 residues. La-Proteina moves to full-atom generation through partially latent flow matching, modeling backbone coordinates explicitly while encoding sequence and variable-length side-chain geometry in per-residue latents. Proteina-Complexa conditions atomistic generation on target structures, pretraining on Teddymer, a synthetic dataset of domain–domain interaction pairs extracted from predicted monomer structures, and applying inference-time search across protein and small-molecule targets.

With collaborators at the Institute for Protein Design and partner institutions, RFD4-Proteina expands this latent-space approach to standard and modified amino acids, glycans, DNA, and RNA under force-field supervision. The model combines intrinsic recurrent refinement in latent space with extrinsic search guided by geometric or energetic constraints. Experimental campaigns validated designs across therapeutic target binders, phosphorylation recognition, glycosylation control, and de novo enzymes catalyzing bond-breaking and bond-forming reactions, including an enzyme for active pharmaceutical ingredient synthesis.

Additional experimental design collaborations at Duke and partner laboratories are ongoing.

Theme 03

Agentic design workflows

Connecting biological mechanisms to computational assays and iterative binder design.

Generative models produce candidate structures and sequences, which is one step toward achieving a specific biological effect. Designing a competitive inhibitor, a multi-partner recruiter, or a conformation-selective binder also requires selecting constructs and target sites, choosing controls, and testing whether candidates satisfy the intended mechanism rather than interface confidence alone.

In BindClaw, we built an agent-assisted workflow that starts from a biological prompt or published paper, identifies relevant target structures and candidate binding sites, and constructs an executable, mechanism-specific computational assay. Operating against fixed assessment criteria and a defined compute budget, agents coordinate multiple design backends, including Proteina-Complexa and RFD4-Proteina, and revise binding sites, generator settings, or candidate sequences in response to computational results.

Across nine targets and 6,480 structural evaluations, BindClaw benchmarks showed that binding-site preferences vary substantially across generators. In a designer-assisted experimental campaign targeting calmodulin, 15 of 18 tested designs bound calcium-loaded calmodulin by biolayer interferometry, including three subnanomolar and nine single-digit nanomolar binders; 13 of those 15 showed no detectable binding under calcium-depleted conditions.

Theme 04

Search and structure at scale

GPU-accelerated homology search and proteome-wide quaternary structure prediction.

Homology search is a major computational step in sequence annotation and structure prediction pipelines. With collaborators, we redesigned gapless prefiltering and gapped profile alignment in MMseqs2 for modern GPU architectures, reaching speeds up to 100 trillion cell updates per second while supporting consumer and low-wattage GPUs.

Fast search makes proteome-wide complex prediction practical. Across 4,777 proteomes spanning model organisms and species relevant to global health, we predicted 31 million candidate homo- and heterodimeric complexes and calibrated confidence criteria against experimentally determined structures, yielding 1.81 million high-confidence predictions in the AlphaFold Database. Within that release, the top 1% of structural clusters account for roughly 44% of all complexes, 8.3% of clusters span multiple domains of life, and 31.3% of high-confidence predictions extend beyond detectable PDB coverage.

In a focused campaign for pandemic preparedness, we predicted monomer and dimer structures across 2,812 viral proteomes from 23 viral families relevant to human health. The campaign produced 5,279 high-confidence heterodimers and 2,749 homodimers consolidated into 1,598 structural interface clusters, of which 471 (29.5%) had no detectable similarity to experimentally determined interfaces in the PDB. Analysis of these assemblies provided structural data on vaccinology-relevant glycoproteins and cleavage-site specificity in viral proteases, released through the AlphaFold Database Pandemic Preparedness Portal.

Theme 05

Benchmarks and evaluation

Testing whether predictive models generalize under realistic experimental splits.

We build benchmarks that evaluate protein fitness predictors under the distribution shifts encountered in protein-engineering campaigns, such as mutational-depth extrapolation and low-homology splits, alongside standard partitions.

FLIP established standardized tasks for thermostability, binding, and viral capsid viability. FLIP2 adds seven curated datasets covering enzymes, protein–protein interactions, and light-sensitive proteins, with documented provenance for each dataset and redistribution under CC-BY 4.0.

Across the FLIP2 datasets and splits, regularized baselines and shallow models frequently matched or outperformed fine-tuned protein language models, delineating practical limits of current transfer-learning strategies for fitness prediction.
